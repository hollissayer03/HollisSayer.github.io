DESIGN DECISIONS

Tool & workflow design

Design challenge

The main goal was to create a large level efficiently using procedural content generation (PCG), then make it possible to rapidly iterate large environments later in production. I wanted a reusable workflow that could respond to layout changes and feedback without rebuilding the scene by hand, while preserving control over terrain, biome placement and foliage.

A test environment for the procedural workflow

I used a hypothetical Scottish inspired landscape of mountains, forests and plains as a test of what the tool could produce. It gave me a varied environment in which to demonstrate efficient level creation and explore how parameter changes could support rapid iteration at a large scale. The scene expressed my ideal environment, but creating that particular world was not the project’s main goal.

The planned moods of its biomes, including challenge in the mountains, curiosity in deciduous forests and unease in conifer forests, provided useful design constraints for testing creative control. They were secondary to evaluating the reusable workflow, rather than a separate game experience or an outcome established through player testing.

Keeping the layout editable

The proposed workflow began with a rough map layout, followed by separate 2D inputs for height and biome placement. A grayscale heightmap described the terrain, while a colour mask assigned the biome regions. Parameters would then let me adjust terrain height, erosion, foliage density and biome distribution without starting again from the original layout.

ITERATION

How the workflow changed

Building a tool around the design

Original plan

I initially planned to combine existing Houdini Digital Assets, including a biome region tool, to build the environment.

Problem & change

The existing biome tool did not offer the precise, editable scene I wanted. I chose to create my own HDA so I could tailor the terrain and biome controls to the project, rather than adapt the design around someone else’s tool.

Result & trade-off

This gave me a reusable tool with more control over the intended output and demonstrated my ability to build the workflow myself. It also increased development time and left less time to refine the scene’s visual quality, including texture blending between biome masks.

Simplifying the inputs and foliage controls

Original plan

The proposal used several custom colours to identify biomes and included a separate HDA for randomising foliage.

Problem & change

During development, I changed the biome inputs to pure red, green and blue values, avoiding an extra colour conversion step. The plains formed the default area where neither height nor RGB biome input was assigned. I also found that foliage randomisation could be handled with a parameter instead of another HDA.

Result

These changes simplified the workflow and removed unnecessary setup. They also exposed gaps in my initial research: I had planned a particular route to the result before fully understanding the alternatives.

Refining the terrain shape

The initial terrain had abrupt, stepped slopes that did not match the natural mountain profile I intended. I refined the shape to create smoother transitions and added surface detail in response to feedback. The comparison shows the initial shape and the later terrain recorded in the diary after smoothing and erosion.

OUTCOME

Outcome & what I learned

What worked

The project produced a reusable tool aimed at efficiently creating large levels and supporting rapid changes later in production. The Scottish inspired scene served as its test environment. Heightfield generation exceeded my initial expectations, and exposed parameters let me regenerate and adjust the scene while retaining control over its layout.

What remained unresolved

Although the demonstration scene was not the main deliverable, it revealed areas where the generated output still needed refinement. It did not reach the visual quality I had planned. Biome texture transitions remained too sharp, ground textures showed repetition, and asset distribution was too uniform. Scenes with many instances could also take several minutes to recook after a parameter change, reducing the speed of iteration as the node network became more complex.

Improving the feedback process

I underestimated the development time and placed too much feedback collection near the end of the schedule. With little time remaining, I could not collect enough data or iterate the scene to its full potential. In future projects, I would test a smaller working version earlier, distribute subject matter expert reviews and general playtesting throughout production, and reserve contingency time for changes.

Next steps

My priorities would be smoother biome texture blending, more varied asset distribution and investigating ways to reduce recooking time. The main lesson was that a reusable tool needs both a controllable workflow and a realistic schedule for evaluating the environments it produces.

Terrain evolution through the node workflow

These diary screenshots show the progression from the imported heightmap through smoothing and erosion, followed by a height based mask for the mountain top.

01 · HeightField File

Importing the grayscale heightmap

The imported heightmap produced abrupt, stepped changes in elevation. This was the starting shape before smoothing.

02 · HeightField Blur

Smoothing the height transitions

I added HeightField Blur and set its radius to 90. The diary records a smoother mountain profile after this change.

03 · HeightField Erode

Adding erosion detail

After feedback, I added HeightField Erode and adjusted its controls to give the mountain surface more natural detail. The diary does not record the exact erosion settings.

04 · HeightField Mask by Feature

Selecting the mountain top

I used height to define a mask for the icy mountain top, shown in red. This stage selects an area for later treatment; it does not change the terrain shape.
