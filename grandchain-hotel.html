PROJECT / 01 · GROUP PROJECT · SHIPPED TITLE

GrandChain Hotel

GrandChain Hotel is a team-developed digital escape room prototype set in an absurdist hotel. Players act as a new employee, using security cameras, a chute system and messages from the manager to complete guest requests. My contributions focused on puzzle design, room-layout diagrams, progression planning and interface concepts, working with other designers, artists and programmers to turn those designs into a playable experience.

MY CONTRIBUTIONSPROBLEM SOLVING IN A TEAM PROJECT

My contributions

Designing the food delivery puzzle

What is the food delivery puzzle?

The player works in the hotel’s operations room and is asked to deliver food to a guest in a numbered room. A chute is a delivery passage that transports an item from the operations room to a guest room. The player uses the computer to choose which chute to activate.

The security cameras show the guest rooms and their chute connections, but not their room numbers. A blueprint on the desk shows the numbered rooms and their layouts, but not which chute serves each room. To solve the puzzle, the player compares the positions of the bed, wardrobe and window in the camera view with the blueprint, identifies the requested room, and sends the food through the matching chute.

Design challenge

For this matching puzzle to work, every room needed a distinct arrangement that the player could recognise from both the camera view and the blueprint. My challenge was to make those layouts unique while giving the team an efficient, consistent way to create them.

Design decision

I created a shared room template with a fixed camera position and predefined locations for the identifying objects. I then listed the possible layout combinations so team members could choose a distinct arrangement and use the same template to design their room. This reduced repeated planning and made it easier to avoid duplicate layouts.

The aim was to make each room distinguishable through its spatial clues, rather than require a completely different set of assets for every room. Keeping those clues visible from the camera was essential to the puzzle.

How the layout supports the puzzle

I designed a matching task that splits the clues between a numbered room blueprint and security-camera views. The cameras identify the chute connections but do not reveal the room numbers. The blueprint identifies the rooms but does not provide the chute labels. Players compare furniture and window placement to identify the requested room, then select its chute to deliver the food.

The intended challenge is deduction through spatial comparison. Incorrect deliveries allow another attempt, keeping the focus on understanding the clues rather than a severe penalty.

Helping the team build consistent rooms

I gave contributors a shared template and combination list so they could allocate distinct layouts before building them. The diagrams and blueprint map provided a common reference for matching the camera views to room numbers, helping the team coordinate the puzzle’s spatial clues.

The shared constraints kept the identifying objects visible and the layouts consistent without requiring every room to be planned independently. Decorative detail could then be added around a dependable puzzle layout.

Room layouts and blueprint iterations

The template turned room creation into a set of shared choices: select an object position, check it against the combination list, and build the layout around the fixed camera. I asked contributors to select combinations before creating their rooms, giving the team a common starting point.

Iterating the identifying objects

The early template used bed, door and window positions. The later puzzle design used bed, wardrobe and window placement as the identifying clues. This changed which objects carried the puzzle information while retaining the same approach of distinct arrangements viewed from a fixed camera.

From prototype to blueprint presentation

My early map arranged the room variations around a corridor. The later iteration used a blueprint visual style and a clear symbol key for windows, beds, wardrobes and cameras. These diagrams supplied the spatial clues that the food-delivery puzzle depended on.

Developing the rat puzzle concept

What was my original concept?

I designed a timed task in which rats escape from a neglected food-storage room through a broken door. The manager unlocks its security-camera feed and asks the player to send food through the chute to distract the rats while he finds an alternative way to close the door.

The player watches the rats emerge from a hole in the wall and decides when to send food. In my original design, each food item deals with one rat: if it arrives before the rat crosses a defined threshold, the rat collects it and returns to the hole. If the rat has already crossed the threshold, it continues toward the hotel.

Timing and success conditions

I specified timed rat releases that gradually speed up over a one-minute sequence. The manager then overrides the door, which closes once the last rat has escaped or returned. My concept allowed up to five rats to escape for a successful outcome; more than five meant failure. This made the timing rule and completion conditions explicit for the implementation team.

Why I designed it this way

The task reused the camera and chute system introduced by the food-delivery puzzle, but shifted the focus from identifying a room to reacting at the right moment. Unlocking another camera also revealed a new part of the hotel.

Feeding an infestation instead of eliminating it was deliberately absurd. The neglected room and the manager’s reluctance to address the problem supported his character and suggested that the hotel’s polished appearance concealed poor care behind the scenes.

Communicating the concept

I wrote the concept as a puzzle explanation, walkthrough, design justification and separate artist and programmer checklists. These covered the room’s visual condition, required props, rat paths, trigger threshold, release timing and success rules, giving each discipline a concrete reference for developing the task.

How the concept changed

These images and the movement diagram show my original food-distraction concept. The later team design changed the interaction to dropping cans to scare rats with noise, with cheese serving as an ineffective choice.

Interface assets and screen layout

I created interface concepts and assets for the camera view, manager messages and chute controls. The visual direction used familiar desktop-window conventions, with blue frames, pale backgrounds and recognisable camera and envelope symbols.

Reusable interface elements

I prepared buttons, frames and icons as a visual starting point for the interface. These images show my design assets, rather than a claim that I programmed the UI.

Bringing the interactions together

My layout placed the camera view, chute controls and manager conversation on one screen. This kept the key interactions visible together and gave the team a concrete composition to work from.

Communicating puzzle behaviour across disciplines

I translated the rat concept into a walkthrough, movement diagram and separate artist and programmer checklists. The programmer checklist specified the trigger threshold, timed rat releases and the response to food; the artist checklist described the neglected storage room and its required props.

I also created a puzzle dependency diagram linking tasks to the manager’s messages and narrative beats. This made the intended progression visible in one place. It represents an earlier, broader plan, rather than every feature in the final prototype.

Reducing confusion in the shared interface

Team problem

Playtesting highlighted confusion about switching cameras, knowing when tasks were complete and moving between applications. Some players did not realise additional camera views were available.

Design response

My combined-screen mockup supported keeping the cameras, chute controls and chat visible together. The team’s revised interface used visible camera-switch controls and clearer task feedback, reducing the need to move between separate applications to check an action’s result.

My role and the team outcome

I contributed interface concepts and design documentation within a shared design process. The playable UI and puzzle systems were implemented collaboratively; I am showing the design assets and planning I produced, rather than attributing the whole implementation to myself.
